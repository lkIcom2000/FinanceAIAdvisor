## Design relevance

- **Contrast with mainstream architectures.** In ReAct (F6-7) and in the surveys' planning/action modules (F2) and brain-perception-action framework (F5), the LLM is the planner that selects tools. The thesis design moves planning and tool selection to a deterministic orchestrator and keeps the LLM in a perception/NLU and phrasing role. That is a deliberate narrowing of the "brain" or "planning module" role. It can be framed as an architectural variant that the survey frameworks do not cover, not as a departure from agent research as such.
- **Autonomy as a design decision (F9).** Feng et al. state that autonomy is a design choice separate from capability, which supports a gate that assigns levels per action. Their five user roles map approximately onto the thesis's levels: inform ≈ Operator, recommend ≈ Collaborator/Consultant, execute after confirmation ≈ Approver, fully autonomous ≈ Observer. The thesis's "mandate + notification + undo" level has no direct counterpart. It sits between Approver and Observer, adding standing delegation and reversibility. This could be a contribution.
- **Per-action, not per-agent.** Feng et al. certify a maximum autonomy per agent (autonomy certificates, F10). The thesis gate assigns a level to each proposed action, using a ceiling plus action-specific downgrades. The certificate idea can be cited as kernel theory for a configured ceiling (for example, a user mandate as the "certificate").
- **Governance practices built into the architecture (F15).** Constraining the action space and requiring approval, default behaviors, legibility, monitoring, attributability and interruptibility map directly onto a deterministic gate (action-space constraint plus approval), a deterministic orchestrator (legible, attributable decisions) and notification with undo (interruptibility, extended to reversal after the fact). Confirm the exact wording against the PDF before citing.
- **Risk grows with autonomy (F11, F13).** Mitchell et al. and Chan et al. justify bounded autonomy. Financial actions score high on directness of impact, so Chan's characteristics can be used as gate criteria: the more direct and irreversible the impact, the lower the allowed level.
